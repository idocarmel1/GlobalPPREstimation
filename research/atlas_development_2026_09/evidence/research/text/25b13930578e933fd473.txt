Hindcasting the food-web dynamics of the tropical Indian Ocean pelagic ecosystem over 
the last two decades 
 
Roger Amate1, Maria José Juan-Jordá 2, Xavier Corrales1, Iker Zudaire3, Eider Andonegi1 
 
SUMMARY 
Tuna species are among the most widely harvested fish stocks globally, contributing approximately 
6.8% of the global finfish catches. In the tropical Indian Ocean , tropical tuna fisheries alone 
account for about  22% of the global tuna catches, however these fisheries  also interact with a 
variety of non-targeted species, including vulnerable taxa . In the ecosystem, fisheries have the 
same ecological role as a  high-trophic level predator,  therefore it is important to monitor h ow 
their activity  may influence the food web -structure and functioning of the oceanic pelagic 
ecosystem over time. In this study , we build upon the Ecopath model presented at the previous 
meeting of the IOTC Working Party on Ecosystems and Bycatch (Amate et al., 2024) , updating 
biomass and discards estimates, refining dietary information, and incorporating expert knowledge 
review. Using this updated Ecopath model, we also developed and fitted a  temporally dynamic 
Ecosim model to time series data from 2003 to 2022. The model reproduced satisfactorily observed 
trends for several functional groups, particularly those with biomass time  series data available . 
For some vulnerable species and mid- to lower-trophic level groups, data limitations constrain the 
model’s capacity to fully capture the historical ecosystem dynamics . Trophic interactions and 
fishing pressure emerge as key drivers of ecosystem change. Notably, results indicate a decline in 
the mean trophic level of the catch and in the biomass of commercially important species  in line 
with t hose of the stock assessment . This study contributes to a deeper understanding of the 
dynamics of the tropical Indian Ocean pelagic ecosystem and marks a step forward in advancing 
the operationalization of the ecosystem approach to fisheries management in the IOTC.

1. Introduction  
Effective fisheries management is essential to ensure the long-term contribution of fisheries  to global 
food security, livelihoods and the cultural identity of coastal communities and fishing nations closely tied 
to tuna resources (Boyd et al., 2022, Robinson et al., 2022) . Among these fisheries, tunas and tuna-like 
species are among the most extensively harvested fish stocks globally, with catches reaching 5.2 million 
tonnes in 20 23 (ISSF, 2025). Among the seven principal market tuna species, 87 percent of their total 
catches come from healthy stocks in terms of  abundance (ISSF, 2025). However, tuna and tuna -like 
fisheries are not limited to catch the target species, they also interact with a variety of non-target species 
that may be incidentally caught as bycatch, including vulnerable taxa such as sharks and sea turtles. The

ecosystems and fisheries through shifts in primary productivity, increasing acidification, and rising

The Indian Ocean is a major fishing ground for tuna fisheries, but the Indian Ocean Tuna Commission 
(IOTC) faces significant challenges. The rapid expansion of both artisanal, semi-industrial and industrial 
fisheries led to catches peaking at 1.7 million tonnes in 2006. Since then, catches have slightly declined 
and have remained below that maximum (Murua et al., 2015). In the tropical Indian Ocean, bigeye tuna 
(Thunnus obesus), yellowfin tuna (Thunnus albacares), skipjack tuna (Katsuwonus pelamis) and small 
tunas (Euthynnus affinis, Auxis thazard, Auxis rochei) dominate the catch, of tuna and tuna-like fisheries. 
However, the catches in tuna fisheries are rarely monospecific, and bycatch of vulnerable species such as 
sharks and sea turtles is common (Huang and Liu, 2010, Ruiz et al., 2018, Elliott et al., 2024)  with the 
bycatch species composition varying by fishing gear . Many of these bycatch species such as billfishes 
and sharks, together with the tunas,  are top predators, playing key roles in regulating the structure and 
functioning of oceanic ecosystems (Kiszka et al., 2022, Dedman et al., 2024). Ecosystem variability due 
to the emerging pressure of climate change, incomplete catch and effort data reporting for some fisheries, 
poor decision -making processes and weak compliance monitoring  are undermining the ability of the 
IOTC to ensure the long-term sustainability of tuna and tuna-like stocks, as well as the ecological integrity 
of the Indian Ocean pelagic ecosystem and the communities that depend on it  (Erauskin-Extramiana et 
al., 2019, Welch et al., 2023).  
IOTC have committed to operationalize the ecosystem approach to fisheries management (EAFM)  
(Andonegi et al., 2019, Juan Jordá, 2019) . This holistic approach to fisheries management aims to 
complement traditional single-species management advice by  incorporating bycatch, ecosystem and 
climate considerations. Within this context, marine ecosystem models are powerful tools as they provide 
a framework to integrate available information on ecosystem components, processes and anthropogenic 
pressures (fishing and climate change), providing a framework to explore system  dynamics and inform 
fisheries management  decisions (Geary et al., 2020) . The Ecopath with Ecosim (EwE) modelling 
framework has been widely applied in both coastal and oceanic  ecosystems (Colléter et al., 2015)  
including large-scale tuna fisheries (Olson and Watters, 2003, Forrestal and Ménard, 2016, Griffiths et 
al., 2018). EwE models are valuable tools for assessing the impact of fishing on marine ecosystems, 
evaluating the sustainability of resource exploitation, and exploring how environmental disturbances —
such as climate change or species invasions —affect ecosystem dynamics.  They also help researchers 
understand the structure and functioning of food  webs, identifying key species and energy flows that 
maintain ecological balance (Corrales et al., 2017, Griffiths et al., 2018, Bornatowski et al., 2018). 
This study presents the first Ecopath with Ecosim model developed specifically for the tropical Indian 
Ocean pelagic ecosystem to support decision -making processes within the Indian Ocean Tuna 
Commission (IOTC). Building on the static  Ecopath model previously presented at the IOTC Working 
Party on Ecosystems and Bycatch  (Amate et al., 2024) , this study incorporates improved biomass and 
discards estimates, updated dietary information, and expert knowledge review. The temporally dynamic 
model Ecosim was then fitted to time series of data from 2003 to 2022, enabling simulation of ecosystem 
responses over time. Using this EwE model, the objective of this study is to improve t he understanding 
of the structure and the functioning of the Tropical Indian Ocean (TIO)  oceanic pelagic ecosystem, by 
characterizing its key trophic interactions and its historical dynamics in response to fishing between 2003 
and 2022. Subsequently, this model will be used to assess the cumulative impacts of fishing on tuna and 
tuna-like species, as well as the broader ecosystem, under both historic and potential future fishing  and 
climate change scenarios. Model outputs will include ecological indicators that connect the impacts of 
fishing and climate change on species and ecosystem structure and functioning to fisheries management 
advice. These outputs will directly  support the current development of EAFM products (e.g. regional 
EcoCard and Ecosystem Fisheries Overviews ) within IOTC , providing managers with a broader 
ecological context for managing tropical tuna species and associated ecosystems  in the tropical Indian 
Ocean.

2. Methods 
2.1. Ecopath model 
The Tropical Indian Ocean (TIO) Ecopath model is a quantitative representation of trophic flows and 
biomasses in the pelagic ecosystem. The model represents the mean state of the ecosystem between 2000 
and 2003 and covers an area of over 21.7 million km2 between 0 and 500 m depth (Figure 1). The model 
represents oceanic waters, excluding the continental shelf from the analysis (except for small islands and 
archipelagos that extend within the study area), since it represents an area of minor importance for tropical 
tuna fisheries and because including it would imply modelling the demersal/benthic ecosystem, as well 
as the inclusion of other more artisanal and national fleets. In both cases, this would have an irrelevant 
impact on the dynamics of the target species in ecological and fishery terms, while simultaneously adding 
greater uncertainty to the model. 
 
Figure 1. Practical model area (in blue). The theoretical area represents the same area of the tropical Indian Ocean, but without 
including the continental shelf (except for small islands and archipelagos). 
The model is composed of 35 functional groups (FG), ranging from primary producers to top predators. 
Functional groups represent ontogenetic stages of a species (e.g., mature and immature), single species, 
or groups of species that share ecological characteristics (e.g., diet)  (Christensen and Walters, 2004) . 
Functional groups of the TIO model were defined according to the commercial importance of the species, 
data availability, and the ecology of the species, such as diet or bathymetric distribution (Table 1). The 
model includes ten groups of vulnerable species (one for seabirds, two for cetaceans, six for 
elasmobranchs, and one for sea turtles). Main target species were modelled separately such as swordfish 
(Xiphias gladius) or albacore tuna (Thunnus alalunga). In addition, the three tropical tuna species: bigeye 
tuna (BET), yellowfin tuna (YFT), and skipjack tuna (SKJ) were separated into two ontogenetic groups 
(i.e., matures and immatures) attaining their length at first maturity for fishery and ecological reasons. 
The model also includes functional groups representing species with the highest incidental catch rates by 
tuna fleets (e.g., silky shark, other sharks, mobulas). 
Estimates of discards were obtained from the literature , except for the Spanish, French and Seychell ois 
purse seine fleets. For these fleets, onboard observers’ data from AZTI and IRD was used to estimate the 
discards in weight (t) of a species or functional group per 1000 tonnes of targeted tuna landed (BET, YFT, 
SKJ). Regarding discard survival, two distinct periods were assumed for these fleets: a period  prior to 
2014 when handling and release best practices were not yet implemented onboard tuna vessels and from 
2014 onwards, when such best practices were being implemented (Jon Ruiz, pers. comm.). For the pre-
2014 period, discard mortality was assumed to be 100%. In contrast, for the period  post-2014, discard 
mortality was estimated based on empirical studies of at -haulback mortality and post -release survival

2.2. Ecosim model 
The static Ecopath model was calibrated and fitted to available time series data from 2003 to 2022 using 
Ecosim. Ecosim is the time-dynamic module of the EwE approach, built on a set of differential equations 
that describe biomass dynamics over time  (Christensen and Walters, 2004, Walters et al., 1997) . These 
equations are derived from the fundamental Ecopath production equation and are expressed as follow: 
𝑑𝐵𝑖
𝑑𝑡 = 𝑔𝑖 ∑ 𝑄𝑗𝑖 − ∑ 𝑄𝑖𝑗 + 𝐼𝑖 − (𝑀0𝑖 + 𝐹𝑖 + 𝑒𝑖) ×  𝐵𝑖 
Where  
𝑑𝐵𝑖
𝑑𝑡   represents the rate of biomass change, Bi, of the functional group i during the time interval 
dt. 𝑔𝑖 is the net growth efficiency, 𝑄𝑗𝑖 is the consumption rate of j by the functional group i, and 𝑄𝑖𝑗 is the 
consumption rate on the functional group  i by the predator  j. 𝐼𝑖 is the immigration rate , while 𝑀0𝑖,
𝐹𝑖,  𝑒𝑖 represent the natural mortality rate, fishing mortality rate, and emigration rate, respectively. When 
multiplied by 𝐵𝑖, these yield the net rates of the three factors. 
Consumption rates (𝑄) are estimated using the “foraging arena theory” (Ahrens et al., 2011). This theory 
is based on the idea that organisms are not constantly exposed to predation risks, as they employ various 
strategies to reduce it, such as hiding, schooling, or performing vertical migrations. The “foraging arena 
theory” defines two states: vulnerable, when the prey is available to predators, and non-vulnerable, when 
the prey is not accessible to predators. In the model this implies the division of the prey’s biomass between 
a vulnerable and a non-vulnerable fraction, and the transfer rate or vulnerability (vij) between the two 
fractions determines the trophic flow between the predator and the prey (Ahrens et al., 2011). This concept 
of vulnerability incorporates density-dependent processes and reflects how close a species or functional 
group is to its carrying capacity. Carrying capacity refers to the maximum population size that a species 
or group can reach without negatively impacting itself due to the depletion of essential resources required 
for its own development (Christensen and Walters, 2004, Christensen et al., 2008) . The default 
vulnerability values (vij = 2) represents a mixed trophic flow. A low value (vij < 2) indicates a “bottom-
up” flow and a situation close r to the carrying capacity, whereas a high value  (vij > 2) suggests a “top-
down” flow and a condition further from the carrying capacity  (Ahrens et al., 2011). For each predator-
prey interaction, the consumption rate is calculated as follows: 
𝑄𝑖𝑗 = 𝑎𝑖𝑗 × 𝑣𝑖𝑗 × 𝐵𝑖 × 𝐵𝑗 × 𝑇𝑖 × 𝑇𝑗 × 𝑀𝑖𝑗 𝐷𝑗⁄
𝑣𝑖𝑗 + 𝑣𝑖𝑗 × 𝑇𝑖 × 𝑀𝑖𝑗 + 𝑎𝑖𝑗 × 𝑀𝑖𝑗 × 𝐵𝑗 × 𝑇𝑗 𝐷𝑗⁄ × 𝑓(𝐸𝑛𝑣𝑓𝑢𝑛𝑐𝑡𝑖𝑜𝑛, 𝑡) 
Where 𝑎𝑖𝑗 is the effective search rate of predator j on prey i, 𝑣𝑖𝑗 is the vulnerability of prey i by predation 
of j, 𝐵𝑖 is the biomass of the prey, 𝐵𝑗 is the biomass of the predator, 𝑇𝑖 is the relative feeding time of the 
prey, and 𝑇𝑗 is the relative feeding time of the predator. 𝑀𝑖𝑗 represents mediation effects, and 𝐷𝑗 denotes 
the handling time of the prey by the predator, which limits the consumption rate. 𝑓(𝐸𝑛𝑣𝑓𝑢𝑛𝑐𝑡𝑖𝑜𝑛, 𝑡) is a 
multiplier factor that accounts for the influence of environmental variables (e.g., temperature). This 
multiplier ranges from 0 to 1 and is derived from the environmental response function, modifying the 
consumption rate (𝑄𝑖𝑗) of the functional group. Specifically, the multiplier is calculated based on the 
intersection between the response function to an environmental factor and the actual conditions of that 
factor, with a maximum value of 1 and decreasing values as the environ mental condition deviates from 
the optimal range for the functional group. 
2.3. Time series 
Time series of fishing effort, fishing mortality, and primary production (PP) were used to drive the model, 
while time series of biomass and catches were used to calibrate the model, comparing model’s predictions 
with observed data (Table 4). 
 
 
7 
 
Biomass time series for target ed species were obtained from fishery stock assessments conducted by 
IOTC. Additionally, outputs from global ocean models were used to derive biomass time series for lower 
trophic level functional groups ( Table 4). Catch time series were calculated based on the official IOTC 
landings database. See Amate et al. (2024) for a more detailed description of the calculations.  
Time series of fishing effort were calculated using the official IOTC catch and effort database or obtained 
from published sources (Table 4, Table 5, Figure 2 – Supplementary material). For the free-school (FSC) 
and setting on objects (FAD) purse seine fleets of Spain, France, and Seychelles, effort time series in sets 
were extracted from published studies and for some years, by personal communication (Table 4). For the 
“Others – FSC purse seine” and 'Others – FAD purse seine' fleets, where no fishing effort data were 
available, effort time series in sets were estimated by weighting the effort of the other three purse seine 
fleets in the model (Spain, France, and Seychelles) according to their total catches  to obtain two CPUE 
time series. The effort  time series were obtained by  dividing the catches of each fleet by the ir 
corresponding CPUE. For the rest of the fleets (defined by flag and gear type) included in the model —
namely baitboat, others, line, gillnet, and longline—an analysis was conducted to assess the quality and 
temporal coverage of effort data reported in the IOTC database. Fleets with the highest catch volumes 
within each fleet  grouping included in the model and with the most reliable effort data were selected 
(Table 5). For the baitboat fleet, 90% of the catches correspond to the Maldivian fleet (Table 5). The effort 
trend of the baitboat fleet was assumed to follow the same pattern as that of the Maldivian baitboat fleet, 
scaled according to total catches. Since the effort of the Maldivian baitboat fleet was reported in trips and 
fishing days in the IOTC catch and effort database, trips  were converted  into fishing days using a 
conversion factor of 1 trip = 1 fishing day (pers. comm .). Regarding the gillnet fleet, the two main 
contributors are the Iranian and Sri Lankan fleets, which together account for nearly 80% of the gillnet 
fleet catches in the model (Table 5). As both fleets report their effort in trips, a CPUE for each fleet was 
calculated and then combined into a weighted CPUE based on their respective catches. Using the total 
catches of the gillnet fleet and the combined CPUE, the data to estimate the effort time series were scaled. 
For the longline fleet, the effort data from  the Chinese, Taiwanese, and Japanese fleets, which together 
represent over 55% of the longline fleet catches in the model were combined. All three fleets report their 
effort in hooks (Table 5). By combining their effort and catch data, a CPUE was derived, which was then 
used to scale the total catches of the longline fleet to approximate its overall effort. For the line and other 
fleets, no effort data were available to calculate their effort time series. Therefore, a relative change in 
effort based on the variation in catches was assumed using the year 2003 as the reference point (Table 4). 
Time series of exploitation rates (catch/biomass) were incorporated for species with available fishery 
stock assessments to represent fishing impacts (Figure 2 – supplementary material).  
In addition, the model includes a time series of primary production (PP) (Table 2; Figure 3 – 
supplementary material), obtained from the Copernicus Global Ocean Biogeochemistry Hindcast product 
(https://doi.org/10.48670/moi-00019), as changes in primary production can have a significant impact on 
the ecosystem. 
To fit the Ecosim model to the observed time series data from 2003 to 2022, we used the stepwise fitting 
procedure described by Scott et al. (2016). In our model, t his procedure estimates various vulnerability 
parameters to improve model calibration by comparing model predictions with observed data, using the 
sum of squares (SS) statistic. The fitting procedure identifies the model that provides the best statistical 
fit based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), which penalizes model over -parameterization 
(Heymans et al., 2016, Mackinson et al., 2009) . The maximum number of vulnerabilities that can be 
estimated is k-1, where k is the number of observed time series (in this case 40 time series of biomass 
and catch). Finally, to choose the best final model, we manually evaluated whether the parameterization 
process produced credible and sensible behavior (Heymans et al., 2016). From the three models with the 
best overall fit, the final model was chosen based on how well it reproduced observed values of the three 
tropical tuna species. 
Once the calibration was completed, changes in the biomass and catch of selected functional groups were 
analysed, considering both their ecological and economic importance. To assess the correlation between 
model outputs (biomass and catches) and time, the Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used.

Finally, to evaluate the uncertainty in Ecopath  input parameters (biomass, production, consumption, 
ecotrophic efficiency, and catches) on Ecosim outputs (biomass, catches, and ecological indicators), the 
Monte Carlo (MC) simulation routine was applied. This routine modifies various Ecopath input 
parameters within the confidence intervals previously defined in the pedigree and checks whether the 
modified values meet the mass-balance criteria. If the criteria are met, the MC routine runs the Ecosim 
model using the adjusted Ecopath configuration and the parameters established during the original model 
calibration. The outputs from the various Ecosim simulations are then used to assess the range of possible 
trajectories in biomass and catches of the functional groups, thereby determining the level of uncertainty. 
Specifically, for this model, 250 Monte Carlo simulations were conducted based on the pedigree index 
previously introduced in the Ecopath model of the TIO to determine the 5th and 95th confidence intervals 
for the Ecosim results. 
2.4. Ecological indicators 
A selection of ecological indicators was used to describe ecological changes in the ecosystem over time. 
To calculate these indicators, the ECOIND and the Ecological Network Analysis (ENA) plug-ins of EwE 
were used (Coll and Steenbeek, 2017). The indicators selected for this analysis were: 
(1) Predatory biomass (t·km⁻²): Biomass of all functional groups with a trophic level (TL) ≥ 3.95. 
This indicator typically declines with increasing fishing pressure (Rochet and Trenkel, 2003). 
 
(2) Commercial species biomass (t·km⁻²) : Biomass of all functional groups subject to catches in 
the model. Biomass falling under certain levels may refer to overfished stocks. 
 
(3) Mean trophic level of the community (mTLco) : Average TL of the entire ecosystem , widely 
used to assess fishing impacts  (Rochet and Trenkel, 2003) . The mTLco typically decreases as 
fishing pressure increases. 
 
(4) Kempton’s Q index: A measure of biomass diversity in the ecosystem for organisms with TL ≥ 
3. High values may indicate a healthy and balanced ecosystem, while declines may  signal 
ecosystem degradation (Kempton and Taylor, 1976). 
 
(5) Mean trophic level of the catch (mTLc): Average TL of the catches across all fleets combined. 
Used to evaluate fishing strategies and their impacts  (Pauly et al., 1998) . mTLc falling under 
certain levels may point to overexploitation of top predators  
 
(6) Mean trophic level of the community > 2 (mTLco > 2): Average TL of all the functional groups 
with a TL higher than 2 and lower than 3.25. Used to track changes in lower trophic levels of the 
ecosystem (Rochet and Trenkel, 2003).  
 
(7) Mean trophic level of the community > 3.25 (mTLco > 3.25): Average TL of all the functional 
groups with a TL higher than 3.25 and lower than 4. Used to track changes in intermediate trophic 
level species of the ecosystem (Rochet and Trenkel, 2003) 
 
 
 
10 
 
(8)  Mean trophic level of the community > 4 (mTLco > 4): Average TL of all the functional groups 
with a TL larger than 4. Used to track changes of top predators (Rochet and Trenkel, 2003).  
The uncertainty associated with the input parameters of Ecopath, as previously described, also enables 
the assessment of uncertainty in ecological indicators (Coll and Steenbeek, 2017).  
 
3. Preliminary results 
3.1. Adjustment of the Ecosim model to the historical period (2003-2022) 
The Ecosim model satisfactorily adjusted biomass predictions to the trends of observed values for most 
target species ( Figure 2). The model has successfully reproduced the observed historical trends of the 
biomass of the blue shark (FG. 4), other billfishes (FG. 11), bigeye mature (FG. 13), bigeye immature 
(FG. 14), yellowfin mature (FG. 15), yellowfin immature (FG. 16), skipjack mature (FG. 17) and albacore 
(FG. 19) as well as small epipelagic fishes (FG. 24). However, the model failed to adequately fit the trends 
of observed biomass values for swordfish (FG. 12), skipjack immature (FG. 18) and small mesopelagic 
fishes (FG. 26) (Figure 2).  
The model predicts declining biomass trends for several functional groups targeted by fisheries (Figure 
2). For example, the biomasses of mature and immature bigeye and yellowfin tuna, other billfishes and 
albacore exhibit a negative trend during the simulated period. Additionally, other functional groups, such 
as mature and immature skipjack tuna and blue shark, exhibit a negative trend at the beginning of the 
simulated period, followed by a stable trend with a slight increase toward the end of the simulation. 
(Figure 2). Similarly, the model predicts significant reductions in most functional groups of vulnerable 
species, such as silky shark, other sharks, mobul as, sea turtles, baleen whales, toothed whales, whale 
shark, and pelagic stingray. However, the uncertainty surrounding the predictions for these  vulnerable 
functional groups is considerably higher (Figure 4 – supplementary material). On the contrary, the model 
predicts stable or even increasing biomass trends for intermediate functional groups like, small tunas and 
other bonitos, jacks, mackerel, runners, trevallies, and scads,  and epipelagic fishes – I, II (Figure 4 – 
supplementary material).  
For the 19 functional groups without time series of biomass observations, the model has also predicted 
biomass trends throughout the entire historical period (Figure 4 – supplementary material). 
The model accurately reproduces the biomass declines observed in the assessments of bigeye and 
yellowfin tuna, reflecting the exploitation experienced by both species during the simulated period. It also 
correctly reproduces the post-2008 stabilization of mature and immature skipjack tuna biomasses (Figure 
2), indicating that their biomasses are approaching the targeted BMSY levels. Additionally, the model 
shows a declining trend for silky shark, other sharks, and mobulas, as these are among the main non -
target functional groups impacted by fishing in the area (Figure 4 – supplementary material).  
Regarding catches, the model satisfactorily fitted most of the trends of observed values for most 
functional groups (Figure 3 ), particularly those with available time series of fishing mortality . 
Specifically, the model reproduced the declining trend in the catches of blue shark, mature and immature 
bigeye tuna, mature yellowfin tuna, and albacore. It also shows an increase in the catches of other 
billfishes, immature yellowfin tuna and mature and immature skipjack tuna (Figure 3). 
Time series of observed catch values were available for 12 of the 19 groups without biomass observations. 
Among these, the model was able to fit the catch time series for the silky shark (FG. 5), epipelagic fishes 
– I (FG. 22) and small epipelagic fishes (FG . 24) (Figure 5 – supplementary material). By contrast, it 
couldn’t fit the catch trends for the baleen whales (FG. 2), toothed whales (FG. 3), other sharks (FG. 6), 
whale shark (FG. 7), pelagic stingray (FG. 8), mobulas (FG. 9), sea turtles (FG. 10), small tunas and other 
bonitos (FG. 20), jacks, mackerels, runners, trevallies and scads (FG. 21), epipelagic fishes – II (FG. 23) 
and large mesopelagic fishes (FG. 25) (Figure 5 – supplementary material). Overall, catch trends of non-
targeted species show an increase in the area due to the general rise in fishing effort throughout the entire 
period (Figure 1 & Figure 5 – supplementary material).

4. Discussion 
This study provides the first comprehensive ecosystem model of the tropical Indian Ocean oceanic pelagic 
ecosystem. Using the Ecopath with Ecosim approach, we integrated species interactions  and the 

 
 
14 
 
ecosystem dynamics with fishing impacts using historical data from 2003 to 2022. The model adequately 
reproduced most observed time series of biomass and catches for most species and functional groups. For 
the three tropical tuna species, the model performs particularly well for mature stages but is less accurate 
for immature stages due to higher variability in the observed data. While other functional groups such as 
albacore, other billfishes, and blue shark are generally well modelled, the swordfish trend is less 
accurately captured. Catch predictions perform well for the ten functional groups with available fishing 
mortality time series but show poorer alignment with catch data for those without such time series. 
Results reveal significant declines in biomass for several top predatory species, in line with some of the 
existing stock assessments. In contrast, species subject to lower fishing exploitation show more stable or 
increasing trends, highlighting differential responses of the functional groups across the food web. These 
findings suggest that human activities such as fishing along with environmental variability not only can 
affect target species but al so cascade through the ecosystem  altering, trophic structure, ecosystem 
functioning, and biodiversity.  
By providing a holistic understanding of ecosystem dynamics and the cumulative effect of fishing on the 
ecosystem within the last 20 years, this work offers critical information to form ecosystem-based advice 
to support the management of tropical tuna species and associated ecosystems in the tropical Indian Ocean 
and guide EAFM implementation.  
Fishing has shaped the biomass trends of tuna and tuna -like species and their associated ecosystem 
differently across the oceanic ecosystem of the tropical Indian Ocean. Species that have been overfished 
in recent years (e.g., other billfishes) generally continue to decline in biomass. In contrast, species that 
haven’t been overfished (maintained above their B MSY) (e.g., swordfish), or that have experienced a 
reduction in fishing mortalities (e.g., blue shark, skipjack tuna) in recent years, show increasing biomass 
trends in the final years of the simulation.  This pattern is attributed to moderate fishing pressure and 
greater resource availability resulting from the decline in competitor abundance. These results seem to be 
in line with those from the assessments probably due to the use of their outputs to fit the model. Vulnerable 
species incidentally caught by various fleets (e.g., silky shark, other sharks, and mobulas) seem to exhibit 
a marked decrease in biomass due to the high mortality rates associated with bycatch events. Overall, 
these results suggest that the ecosystem may be negatively impacted by fishing, particularly at higher 
trophic levels and with clear effects on certain species. However, with appropriate resource management, 
population stability and recovery could be achievable.  
Ecological indicators reflect further the impacts of fishing and trophic changes in the ecosystem. The 
decline in the biomass of commercial species at higher trophic levels, as well as the decrease in the mean 
trophic level of the catch may suggest an increasing proportion of immatures being caught relative to 
matures, or a shift towards harvesting species from lower trophic levels. The slight decrease in the mean 
trophic level of low-trophic-level species suggests a top-down trophic control—the decline of predators 
allows for an increase in mid-trophic-level species, which in turn leads to a reduction in the trophic levels 
immediately below. Furthermore, the pronounced decline in the Kempton’s Q index, suggest a sustained 
reduction in species diversity within the ecosystem. These results coincide with those obtained for the 
historical period in the Ecosim model of the Western Pacific Warm Pool Province (Griffiths et al., 2018) 
revealing a reduction in species diversity at the higher trophic levels. Although in both cases the catches 
of target species decrease, in the Pacific they are replaced by species of similar trophic level, whereas in 
the Indian Ocean they are substituted by species of lower trophic level. However, in both scenarios, the 
replacement species are of no commercial interest to the fishery at present.  
Ecosystem models are limited by availability and quality of data, which is the principal factor introducing 
uncertainty in model outcomes. Key challenges  on information gaps involved collecting biomass 
estimates, catch and effort data and diet composition , which are basic  input data for the model.  This 
highlights the lack of biological and fisheries data in oceanic regions, particularly within the study area, 
and therefore, the need to increase their collection. 
Biomass estimates are scarce across all trophic levels within the study area, particularly for species not 
targeted by major fisheries. This includes apex predators as well as pelagic invertebrates, and it limits the 
model’s ability to fit time series data. Therefore, future efforts should focus on estimating and monitoring 
 
 
15 
 
the biomass of key species or functional groups in the ecosystem, using methods such as acoustic 
detection, pelagic fishing surveys, plankton sampling, or onboard observer programs.  Dietary 
composition data were also insufficient  often based on limited samples  or highly localized studies in 
specific areas and time periods, making the data unrepresentative of the species as a whole or the intended 
temporal scope. This lack of high-quality biomass, catch and diet information becomes more pronounced 
for specie s at intermediate and lower trophic levels  being generally poorly studied. To accurately 
characterize ecosystem structure and interactions, studies on the trophic ecology of species are essential. 
Given that stomach content analysis is complex and time-consuming, alternative methods such as DNA-
based ana lysis of stomach contents may also offer a promising approach to deciphering the trophic 
ecology of species (Gunther et al., 2021, Canals et al., 2024). 
Model calibration, validation, and uncertainty analysis are essential to ensure reliable model performance 
management, quality and relevance. The model developed in this study incorporates the best available 
information and follows best practices for ecosystem model construction and calibration (Heymans et al., 
2014). For the development of the Ecopath TIO model, the PREBAL analysis (Link, 2010) was applied 
to ensure consistency of the basic input parameters with general ecological principles and to guide the 
balancing process. Nevertheless, the model and its projections present certain limitations as previously 
discussed. Although the model includes time series of biomass, catches, fishing mortality for key 
predators, and fleet s’ fishing effort, some of these time series  have been derived based on significant 
assumptions. For example, blue shark, swordfish, and albacore tuna are present within the modelled area 
but their distribution ranges are broader and include the temperate zone of the Indian Ocean. Their 
dynamics within the model may therefore represent the tropical component of their populations but may 
not reflect the entire stock dynamics A further limitation concerns fishing effort data for multiple fleets. 
Many fleets report effort inconsistently, only for certain years, in partial coverage or in  different effort 
units and some do not report effort at all. These inconsistencies make it difficult to realistically estimate 
trends and approximate the variation in fishing effort across fleets. In addition, the model does not include 
a parameter to account for the unquantified variability in fishing efficiency over time due to the adoption 
of various adjustments in fishing practices—commonly referred to as 'effort creep'. Failing to account for 
this technological improvement may result in higher catch rates not being properly quantified, t hereby 
compromising the reliability of the effort time series and the model’s outputs. 
The model also faces limitations in estimating bycatch and its subsequent fate . Although, the IOTC 
nominal catch database  is intended to include both landings and discards across all fleets, in practice, 
most fleets report only their landings. Consequently, bycatch rates  had to be inferred from published 
literature, and for a few fleets , estimated using onboard observers’ data. This approach presents several 
limitations. The main limitation is that, in the absence of bycatch data , or in cases where there are 
significant differences in data quality , the model fails to reflect the actual impacts of certain fleets on 
vulnerable species and the ecosystem. This leads to a bias in the representation of bycatch effects, 
disproportionately attributing greater impact to fleets that report bycatch more compreh ensively. This 
issue is particularly rele vant in the case of purse seine fisheries using FADs, which have received 
considerable attention due to concerns about their ecological impacts, have been extensively studied, and 
for which bycatch data is largely available . However, it is essential to contextualize these concerns by 
comparing bycatch rates across different fishing gears. Empirical data indicate that FAD-associated purse 
seine fisheries often exhibit lower bycatch rates than other methods such as longline or driftnets (Garcia 
& Herrera., 2018; Murua et al., 2021 ). Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation of fishing fleets impacts 
should incorporate bycatch estimates within a reasonable level of uncertainty alongside catch efficiency 
for target species. Another limitation of the approach is that the estimates are expressed as a proportion 
of the target species catches for a specific fleet, year, or period. Applying these rates across the entire 
simulated period requires the assumption that bycatch rates remain constant over time. Additionally, there 
are limitations in estimating the discards’ fate. To address this, we calculated the proportion of discards 
that results in mortality based on studies of at-haulback and post-release mortality. These studies ar e 
scarce and typically cover specific fleets and time periods, with limited sample sizes. Therefore, we 
assume that the proportion of bycatch mortality remains constant throughout the simulation, except for 
the Spanish, French, and Seychellois fleets. This assumption holds despite the fact that the applied value 
is representative of a specific period only, and notwithstanding ongoing efforts to enhance bycatch 
 
 
16 
 
survival through improved release practices. Assuming two distinct periods for discard survival rates —
one prior to the implementation of best practices and another following it— with no transitional phase  
introduces substantial variability in catch estimates for the Spanish, French, and Seychellois purse seine 
fleets. This variability can reach orders of magnitude for functional groups whose catch time series rely 
primarily or exclusively on discards (e.g.,  baleen whales, whale sharks, pelagic stingrays, mobulas) . 
Consequently, for functional groups lacking a fishing mortality time series, particularly those for which 
the adoption of best practices has significantly improved survival rates  (Grande et al., 2019, Grande et 
al., 2022, Stewart et al., 2024), the model encounters difficulties in fitting the catch time series due to the 
abrupt change in the data.  Nevertheless, for some functional groups such as the silky shark, the model 
was able to fit their catch time series despite these concerns. 
Updating and refining the model will improve its value for management and future ecosystem 
assessments. As new information becomes available, the current EwE models can be updated and further 
developed, thereby enhancing their quality and increasing their value as tools for understanding impacts 
and exploring management options for the tropical Indian Ocean pelagic ecosystem.  In the SPC and 
IATTC EwE models are being used to  explore alternative fishing strategies and to assess the combined 
impacts of fishing and climate change on key species  (Griffiths et al., 2021, Allain et al., 2007) . These 
models provide valuable ecosystem-level insights that complement traditional approaches and inform 
discussions on sustainable fisheries governance. 
 
5. Conclusions and future steps 
Being able to model the tropical Indian Ocean ecosystem and its constituent organisms under the 
influence of fishing over the past two decades represents a significant step towards providing ecosystem-
based advice for supporting EAFM implementation in IOTC. This study has developed an Ecopath with 
Ecosim model of the tropical Indian Ocean oceanic pelagic ecosystem to improve understanding of the 
ecosystem’s dynamics, species interactions and the impact of fishing on the ecos ystem structure and 
functioning. Results reveal variations in the biomass  of higher trophic level species, with the greatest 
variations observed in those of highest commercial interest. The findings corroborate the results of the 
assessments and the fact that implementing the scientific advice may lead to improvements in biomas s. 
Moreover, the study also emphasizes the ecosystem’s complexity and the intricate trophic interactions 
within it, reinforcing the need to incorporate these dynamics into marine resource management in IOTC. 
Further development of the model will allow more realistic fits and projections under a wide range of 
management scenarios. Future work could focus on incorporating technological advancements for each 
fleet or fishing gear type to more accurately simulate fleet effort trends. Additionally, to better understand 
the impacts of fishing fleets on vulnerable bycatch species, and in collaboration with the IOTC Secretariat, 
efforts will be made to identify the species considered vulnerable in the Indian Ocean. This will enable 
the development of indicators to assess the effects of fishing on vulnerable species and to certain taxa 
groups. Moreover, work will be carried out to incorporate the effects of clim ate change allowing for 
simulations of future scenarios under varying conditions of climate change, fishing pressure, and their 
combined effects, and to analyse the responses at ecosystem and species -level. Finally, to expand the 
range of scenarios and impacts that can be analysed, a time series will be developed to reflect the reduction 
in bycatch of certain species resulting from the reduction in FAD numbers and improvements in their 
design. These improvements will enhance the model’s capacity to produce more reali stic outcomes and 
to simulate possible future management scenarios. This will make it a more relevant and reliable tool for 
informing fisheries management, incorporating ecosystem considerations to guide EAFM 
implementation within IOTC.
