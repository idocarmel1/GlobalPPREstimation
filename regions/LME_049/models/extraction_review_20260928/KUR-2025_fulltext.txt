A B S T R A C T
The Northwest Pacific (NWP) is not only the region with the highest fishery production in the world, but also the 
location of the Kuroshio-Oyashio Extension (KOE). Regular monitoring and assessment of marine ecosystem are 
essential for maintaining its stability and the sustainability of fishery products. Based on the survey data from the 
high-seas fishery resources survey in the KOE in 2023, the trophic structure and characteristics of open ocean 
ecosystem in this area were analyzed using the Ecopath with Ecosim model. A total of 25 functional groups were 
defined based on their ecological characteristics. The field sampling information was used as the biomass data to 
maximize the quality of the model. The results indicated that the trophic levels of these groups ranged from 1.00 
to 4.35. Chub mackerel is a major economic species in the Northwest Pacific Ocean, and the ecotrophic efficiency 
of chub mackerel was lower than in previous studies (0.74 in 2019), indicating that the proportion of its pro­
duction contributing to the ecosystem energy might have decreased. Mixed trophic impact results showed that 
the main interactions between functional groups are primarily predation and feeding competition. Total primary 
production/total respiration (TPP/TR), and total primary production/total biomass (TPP/TB) indicated the 
system maturity. Compared to previous studies in the KOE and its adjacent area ecosystems (the total system 
throughput was 3529.54 t⋅km-2⋅year-1 and the TR was 486.42 t⋅km-2⋅year-1), both the TST (1766.22 t⋅km-2⋅year-1) 
and the TR (151.27 t⋅km-2⋅year-1) were relatively lower, which revealed the low productivity characteristic of the 
KOE ecosystem. Combined with the high TPP/TR (7.31) and the low system omnivory index (0.20), these overall 
ecosystem characteristics suggested that the KOE ecosystem was structurally simple and vulnerable to distur­
bance from external activities. Sensitivity analysis indicated that the main output parameters of the model were 
highly sensitive to the changes in the biomass of the functional groups. This study provided a reference for 
ecosystem-based fisheries management in the open ocean waters of the KOE.
1. Introduction
The Northwest Pacific (NWP), situated in the East Asian monsoon 
region, is significantly influenced by the monsoon and features a com­
plex ocean current system (Fig. 1). The North Equatorial Current (NEC) 
flows westward, while its eastern offshoot, the Kuroshio Current (KC), 
and the Oyashio Current (OC) to the north converge with the KC to form 
the Kuroshio-Oyashio Extension (KOE) (Tomita and Kubota, 2005; Ji, 
2013). The nutrients carried by the monsoon currents provide abundant 
nourishment, supporting a highly productive ecosystem. As a result, the 
Northwest Pacific (NWP) is one of the most productive fishing zones 
identified by the International Food and Agriculture Organization 
(FAO), accounting for approximately a quarter of global fish production 
(FAO, 2023). Landings in the NWP have been declining annually since 
peaking in the 1990s, with a continuous downward trend in recent years 
(FAO, 2022). How to more effectively protect fishery resources in the 
NWP has become a major concern of researchers and managers. Ac­
cording to the ecosystem-based fisheries management (EBFM), fluctua­
tions of fishery resources can impact other organisms in the ecosystem to 
varying degrees through complex trophic interactions. Declines in 
landings of fishery target species may cause fluctuations in stocks of 
major forage fish species such as mackerel and sardines in the NWP and

monitored to better understand its dynamics. This monitoring could 
provide a baseline reference for local fisheries management organiza­

Ecosystem models, such as Ecopath with Ecosim (EwE), are key tools 
in the implementation of EBFM. These models are based upon the 
thermodynamic, mass-balanced trophic exchange between living groups 
and thus offer a robust, quantifiable platform for marine ecosystem 
research and assessment (Mace, 2001). Compared to single-species 
models, ecosystem models can more realistically reflect the dynamics 
of marine ecosystem. These models account for a range of ecological 
processes and mechanisms, such as trophic interactions among species, 
changes in environmental conditions in the habitat, and ecosystem 
structure (Plag´anyi, 2007). The EwE model is not only user-friendly and 
requires minimal data, which has led to its extensive application in 
marine ecosystem studies, but it also offers valuable insights for inter­
disciplinary research areas such as water resource management and 
climate change. This model has proven-to-work routines and practices 
that can be effectively scaled up and adapted to various contexts 
(Jakeman et al., 2024). For example, the extension of Ecopath in the R 
language, i.e., Rpath, has enhanced the model’s functionality and 
applicability (Lucey et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024). With the develop­
ment of EwE, hundreds of marine ecosystem models have now been 
established worldwide. The number of ecosystem models for the NWP 
has also gradually increased (e.g., Murase et al., 2016; Watari et al., 
2019; Booth et al., 2020). However, most of these studies have focused 
on the Pacific coast, while the structure and function of open ocean 
ecosystems in the NWP, particularly in the KOE, remain insufficiently 
studied.
The accuracy of model parameters is a primary measure of the 
quality of ecosystem models (Ainsworth and Walters, 2015). However, 
due to the scarcity of ocean survey data, biomass parameters in 
ecosystem models are often difficult to obtain through direct sampling. 
Instead, these parameters were frequently estimated using data from 
other models or stock assessment studies, reports from fisheries man­
agement organizations, or even the Ecopath model itself (Heymans 
et al., 2016; Murase et al., 2016).
Based on investigations conducted by the research vessel Songhang 
from Shanghai Ocean University, we have a series of biomass data that 
can be used to replace reference or estimated data in the model con­
struction process. We hope this data will enhance the representativeness 
of the ecosystem model and minimize the uncertainty as much as 
possible. This study focuses on the open ocean ecosystem of KOE, which 
is a major area of fishery disturbance in the NWP. The Ecopath model of 
the KOE was developed using trawl data from the comprehensive 
investigation conducted by the Songhang. The objectives of this study 
are: (1) compare with historical findings to assess the change of KOE 
ecosystem structure and the degree of vulnerability to disturbance, (2) 
analyze the impact of uncertainty in the main model input parameters 
on model-derived indices, and (3) to provide a reference for further 
EBFM in this region.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Sampling area and data sources
The study area was located in the NWP, and close to the exclusive 
economic zone (EEZ) of Japan. Range of the study area were 148◦E to 
164◦E and 35◦N to 45◦N (Fig. 1). A total of 76 fixed survey stations were 
established in this area. The investigation was conducted using the 
research vessel “Songhang”, with a gross tonnage of approximately 3000 
t The survey was conducted from June to August 2023, covering a total 
of 46 days. Biological samples were collected using a fine-mesh pelagic 
trawl equipped with a four-panel net. The dimensions of the trawl were 
434 × 97.1 m, consisting of a 40 mm stretch cod-end mesh and a single 
capsule structure. Due to workload limitations of the vessel, 36 uni­
formly distributed "investigative stations" from the total 76 designed 
trawl survey stations were sampled. In each trawl survey station, the 
sampling duration was approximately1–2 h, with a trawling speed of 
approximately 3–4 knots. A trawl sounder was applied to monitor in­
formation on trawl deployment position, trawl mouth opening size, as 
well as other relevant details during trawling. The trawl depths range 
from 50 m to 150 m. All catches (mainly fish and cephalopods) were 
classified and counted, with the total weight of each species was 
measured on-site.

2.2. Ecopath model
The Ecopath model defines an ecosystem as composed of a set of 
functional groups, each consisting of species with similar ecological 
characteristics. It follows thermodynamic principles to study the 
ecosystem energy flow and network analysis (Plag´anyi, 2007). Func­
tional groups may include species with the same ecological habits, or 
they may include species that are economically or ecologically similar. 
Functional groups may also be used to represent different size or age 
groups of these species. The full set of functional groups should cover all 
major energy flow pathways through the ecosystem (Christensen and 
Pauly, 1992; Heymans et al., 2016). Ecopath estimates the linkage of 
trophic mass-balance using biomass (B), production/biomass (P/B), 
consumption/biomass (Q/B), ecotrophic efficiency (EE), and diet 
composition data as input parameters (Christensen et al., 2005; Hey­
mans et al., 2016; Lucey et al., 2020; Taghavimotlagh et al., 2021). The 
basic equation for model construction was expressed as:

2.3. Functional groups
The aquatic organisms in the KOE ecosystem were classified into 25 
functional groups: 1 detritus group (particulate organic matter, POM), 1 
primary producer group (phytoplankton), 1 zooplankton group, 18 fish 
groups, 2 cephalopoda groups, and 2 crustacean micronekton groups. 
The composition of each group is listed in Table 1.
2.4. Basic input parameters quality
Biomass estimates for functional groups were derived from the 2023 
Songhang survey in 2023, literature review, and other relevant studies 
in the KOE and adjacent areas. The swept area method was used to es­
timate the biomass of most fish species using data from the trawl survey 
in the KOE area, 2023. The swept area is obtained by multiplying the 
effective width of the fishing gear by the distance traveled by the fishing 
vessel during the operation. The biomass per unit area is then calculated 
as the ratio of the total catch to the swept area. However, due to limi­
tations in gear selectivity, some fish, such as tunas, could not be effec­
tively sampled. Consequently, the biomass of tunas was estimated based 
on the latest report released by International Scientific Committee (ISC) 
(ISC, 2023). The biomass of phytoplankton was estimated from survey 
data, while the biomass of zooplankton and detritus was referenced to 
relevant studies. Specific data and parameter sources for the model were 
detailed in the Supplementary Table S1.
Consumption rates, Q/B, were derived from an empirical function 
based on caudal fin profile (Palomares and Pauly, 1989) and obtained 
from FishBase (https://www.fishbase.org) and from other relevant 
studies. Production rates, P/B, were obtained from relevant studies or 
estimated based on the Production/consumption (P/Q) proposed by 
Christensen et al. (2005). Diet composition data were obtained from 
recent studies analyzing stomach contents in the same or adjacent areas. 
Quantitative diet data in terms of wet weight was used for most func­
tional groups, while diets for some groups were described qualitatively. 
The fraction of functional group production used within the living food 
web, ecotrophic efficiencies (EE), were estimated by the model. Due to 
gear selectivity limitations, accurately estimating the biomass of some 
smaller sized organisms were challenging. The biomass of these groups 
was estimated by Ecopath model (Christensen et al., 2005) assuming 
that EE for these groups (ocean sunfish, small mesopelagic fish, small 
pelagic fish, anchovy, Pacific saury, cephalopoda and small cepha­
lopoda) was 0.95. Specific data and parameter sources for the model 
were detailed in the Supplement material (Supplementary Table S1, 
Table S2).
2.5. General ecosystem indices
Mixed trophic impact analysis (MTI) was utilized to evaluate the net 
positive or negative between all functional groups within a stable 
ecosystem (Ulanowicz and Puccia, 1990; Christensen et al., 2005). MTI 
was calculated by constructing an n× n matrix, where i and j represent 
the interactions between impacted group i and impacting group j: 
MTIij = DCij −FCij 
where DCij is the diet composition term which expresses how much 
species i contributes to the diet of j, while FCij represents the feeding 
proportion of i as a predator on j.

the overall condition of the open ocean KOE ecosystem (Christensen 
et al., 2005; Odum, 1969): (1) Total system throughput (TST, 
t⋅km-2⋅year-1), total exports, total respiration, and total biomass indi­
cated the size of an ecosystem; (2) Net system production (NSP), total 
primary production/total respiration (TPP/TR), and total primary

2.6. Pre-balancing and balancing analyses
PREBAL provides a set of diagnostic tools based on ecological and 
fisheries principles to check the reasonableness of input data and ensure 
that key parameters are ecologically viable before the model is balanced, 
and it can provide an important basis for model balancing and subse­
quent analysis (Link, 2010; Heymans et al., 2016). For example, PREBAL 
checks production to biomass (P/B) ratios to ensure that they are 
ecologically appropriate (the P/B ratios of predators are typically lower 
than those of their prey). In accordance with the second law of ther­
modynamics, PREBAL checks the P/Q ratio to ensure that it is between 
0.1 and 0.3 (Christensen et al., 2005; Darwall et al., 2010).
The pedigree index provides an error range on parameters which can 
be used in Monte Carlo resampling based on a semi-quantitative ordinal 
score of data quality (Funtowicz and Ravetz, 1990; Christensen et al., 
2005). Each source of input parameters for each functional group 
required allocation of a different ‘quality score’. Each score was allo­
cated a specific (and default) Confidence Interval, CI (±10–90 % CI). 
Default CIs were obtained from the pre-defined pedigree tables provided 
in Christensen et al. (2005) (Supplementary Table S3) and were uti­
lized to generate parameter-specific pedigree indices, or CI, for B, P/B 
and Q/B (Supplementary Table S4).
2.7. Sensitivity analysis
The Monte Carlo method was applied to analyze the impact of un­
certainty in the main model input parameters (biomass, P/B, and Q/B of 
the functional groups) on model-derived indices (TST, total production 
(TP), net primary production (NPP), TPP/TR, and TPP/TB). First, 
different levels of uncertainty for the input parameters (10 %, 25 %, 40 
% for biomass; 10 %, 20 %, and 30 % for P/B and Q/B) were set based on 
the CI of input parameters sources for each functional group (Morissette 
L., 2007). Then the simulated values were randomly resampled from a 
mean distribution ranging from [(1-uncertainty level) × original input 
value, (1+uncertainty level) × original input value] each time. The re­
sults of corresponding model projections were then estimated and 
compared. This process was repeated 1000 times. Since changes in input 
parameters could lead to an unbalanced situation, only resampled 
values that satisfied the model’s balance conditions (based on the EE<1) 
were selected for subsequent analysis (the range, proportion, and CV of 
successful test parameters were recorded simultaneously). Then we 
calculated the range of the model-derived indices through uncertainty 
analysis. Impacts were assessed as changes (relative bias) to 
model-derived ecosystem indices:

Ecopath with Ecosim 6.6.8 software. ArcMap 10.2 was used to plot the

3. Results
3.1. Food web and trophic level characteristics
Trophic levels of functional groups in the 2023 KOE ecosystem 
ranged from 1.00 to 4.35 (Table 2). The blue shark had the highest 
trophic level at 4.35, followed by other sharks, swordfish, and tunas, 
with trophic levels of 4.34, 4.30, and 4.30, respectively. Among the fish, 
the lowest trophic level was for ocean sunfish (2.66). In addition to most 
fish groups, cephalopoda and small cephalopoda were also in the trophic 
level higher than 3 as secondary consumers. The second trophic level 
includes ocean sunfish, other crustaceans, krill, and zooplankton 
(Fig. 2).
The ecotrophic efficiency (EE) of all functional groups was less than 
1, with the lowest EE for the detritus (0.01), followed by blue shark 
(0.05), zooplankton (0.06), salmon (0.11), Pomfrets (0.13), other sharks 
(0.13), swordfish (0.16) and chub mackerel (0.17). The EE of the 
remaining functional groups was all greater than 0.3. Omnivory indices 
for each functional group ranged from 0 to 0.79. Cephalopoda had the 
highest omnivory indices, followed by small cephalopoda (0.58) and 
large mesopelagic fishes (0.53), while the omnivory indices of all other 
functional groups were below 0.5.
3.2. Mixed trophic impact analysis
Mixed trophic analyses revealed the net effects of changes among 
both direct and indirect relationships on each functional group (Fig. 3). 
As primary producers in the ecosystem, phytoplankton had positive ef­
fects on most of the functional groups, while zooplankton had similar 
effects. In general, most functional groups exerted pronounced positive 
effects on their predators and direct negative effects on their prey 
functional groups (e.g., cephalopoda vs sharks, zooplankton vs phyto­
plankton). Additionally, some functional groups showed negative effects 
due to similar food composition (e.g., chub mackerel vs small pelagic 
fish, Pacific sardines vs Pacific saury). As primary prey items, krill, 
zooplankton, phytoplankton and POM experienced negative impacts 
from other functional groups, with small pelagic fish and anchovy 
having higher impacts on them. Some functional groups had a minor 
impact on others due to their relatively low biomass, such as pelagic 
stingray, large pelagic fish and pomfret.
3.3. General ecosystem indices
In Ecopath models, general ecosystem characteristics are described 
by a series of ecological indices that reflect system size, stability, 
maturity, and so on. The total system throughput (TST) is the sum of all 
flows in a system. It is estimated as the sum of four components (total 
consumption, total export, total respiration and total flows to detritus) of 
the flows. TST of the 2023 KOE ecosystem was 1766.22 t⋅km-2⋅year-1, of 
which the sum of all flows into detritus accounted for 37.99 % (670.92 
t⋅km-2⋅year-1). The sum of all consumption (500.07 t⋅km-2⋅year-1) was 
the next highest, accounting for 28.31 % of the total system throughput. 
Calculated total net primary production (NPP) was 1105.50 t⋅km-2⋅year- 
1, which was considerably larger than the sum of all respiratory flows, 
resulting in the TPP/TR greater than 1. The TPP/TB ratio was 55.00, 
while the system omnivory index (SOI) was 0.20. Nutrient flow in the 
KOE ecosystems had a high proportion of contribution from food chains 
with phytoplankton as the base nutrient source. Phytoplankton 
contributed 74 % of the energy in the KOE ecosystem, while 26 % came 
from POM detritus (Table 3).

However, due to the lack of extensive field survey data, ecosystem-level 
evaluations have been infrequent in the KOE, which is an important 
fishery area. This limitation undoubtedly hinders the implementation of 
ecosystem-based fisheries management. Therefore, using biomass sur­
vey data from the KOE, we constructed an Ecopath model to assess the 
trophic structure and energy flow of the KOE ecosystem in 2023. 
Generally, compared to the trophic structure of other marine ecosystem 
in the KOE (Murase et al., 2016; Watari et al., 2019), the range of trophic 
levels of functional groups in this open ocean area was narrower. For 
example, the trophic levels of most small pelagic fishes, such as chub 
mackerel, Pacific saury, and Pacific sardine, were higher than those in 
other studies, while trophic levels of some top predators (sharks and 
tunas) were lower than other studies. Furthermore, variations in func­
tional groups, diet, and parameters (P/B, Q/B) may also be responsible 
for these findings, which warrant further thorough investigation to 
elucidate their underlying mechanisms.
Ecotrophic Efficiency (EE) is commonly used to indicate the utili­
zation efficiency of functional groups (Heymans et al., 2016). EE for 
most small fishes and cephalopoda was relatively high, similar to find­
ings in other KOE studies (Murase et al., 2016; Watari et al., 2019). 
However, the EE of the main fishery target species (0.17), chub mack­
erel, was lower than previous studies in Watari et al. (2019) (0.74 in 
2019) but similar to the result of Murase et al. (2016). This indicates that 
the production of chub mackerel contributes a lower proportion of en­
ergy and consumption to the KOE ecosystem. And this result may be 
caused by the higher biomass of the chub mackerel compared to the 
other fish functional groups in this model. But it should be note that the 
biomass of chub mackerel in this study was lower than that of previous 
studies (Murase et al., 2016; Watari et al., 2019). The low EE of chub 
mackerel may also be due to unmodelled mortality, which is commonly 
considered to be natural mortality in addition to predation mortality and 
fishing mortality (Christensen et al., 2005; Heymans et al., 2016). 
Meanwhile, since the chub mackerel was a migratory specie, the low EE 
value was also potentially due to partial fishing mortality and predation 
mortality occurring outside the study area. The above reasons derive 
from the fact that mortality caused by predation and fishing is relatively 
weak compared to its own natural mortality. The utilization of chub 
mackerel in the KOE ecosystem could be sub-divided as 36.09 % from 
fishery catch and 63.91 % from predation. As a mid-trophic level fish 
with high abundance, chub mackerel not only has high economic value 
but also serves as prey for many top predators, which explains why its EE 
is dominated by predation. Compared with the results from other Eco­
path model, the fishing mortality of chub mackerel was lower in this 
study (Watari et al., 2019). Even though chub mackerel in the KOE

Additionally, some higher trophic level carnivores, such as sharks

EE of sharks is primarily due to their low predation and fishing 
mortalities.
4.2. Mixed trophic impact analysis
Mixed trophic impact (MTI) analysis was employed to examine the 
direct and indirect interactions among functional groups within the 
ecosystem food webs (Taghavimotlagh et al., 2021). The MTI results 
indicated that the main interactions between functional groups are 
primarily predation and competition. Most functional groups had 
considerable negative impacts on themselves due to intraspecific 
competition arising from similar food composition and resource 
competition. Phytoplankton, zooplankton, and krill, as common basic 
food organisms, had considerable positive impacts on most functional 
groups. These prey organisms, dominated by phytoplankton as the pri­
mary energy source, drive a considerable portion of the energy flow in 
the ecosystem. After organisms are transformed into detritus in open 
ocean areas, only a small portion remains in the ecosystem as POM. 
Production and most trophic pathways in the KOE ecosystem appear to 
be largely driven by food chains with phytoplankton as the base energy 
source.
Most interactions among fish at the mid-trophic level were negative. 
These interactions might arise from feeding competition among fish at 
similar trophic levels (e.g., the impact of small mesopelagic fish on an­
chovy and the impact of chub mackerel on small pelagic fish), or from 
predation (e.g., the impact of chub mackerel on anchovy). Considerable 
negative impacts of fishing on higher trophic level fish (e.g., sharks, 
tunas, pelagic stingray, large pelagic fish) were evident. According to the 
top-down control theory, top predators are selectively removed from the 
water by fishing (Song et al., 2007).
A limitation of this study is that it does not account for the role of 
marine microbes in the chemical cycling of nutrients in ecosystems. 
Microbes play a crucial role in regulating various bio-element trans­
formations and bioremediation processes (Mcquatters-Gollop et al., 
2024). This study primarily focused on high trophic level ecosystems 
and did not incorporate microbial function groups. In the future, we will 
include a broader range of functional groups to enhance the complete­
ness of ecosystem model construction.

which was lower in scale compared with the TST reported in the 2019 
KOE study (3529.54 t⋅km-2⋅year-1) (Watari et al., 2019). This may sug­
gest that the KOE ecosystem scale was lower than previous years, but 
also reflects the fact that the two KOE models cover somewhat different 
study areas. Our survey stations were located in oceanic waters far 
offshore, characterized by a relatively simple ecosystem structure and 
function, and less impacted by land-based anthropogenic activities. 
Ecosystem maturity reflects the complexity and stability of an ecosystem 
in terms of its structure and function. Mature ecosystems typically have 
higher species diversity, complex trophic relationships, efficient energy 
utilization, and strong resilience (Christensen et al., 2005; Odum, 1969). 
Net system production (NSP) is the difference between total net primary 
production (TPP) and sum of all respiratory flows (TR), which indicates 
a higher ecosystem maturity as the value approaches 0 (Christensen 
et al., 2005). The Total primary production/total respiration ratio 
(TPP/TR) is another key indicator of ecosystem maturity. The closer the 
value of TPP/TR is to 1, the more mature the ecosystem is, where all 
production is utilized, leaving no excess available for reuse (Odum, 
1969). The TPP/TR of the KOE ecosystem model in 2023 was 7.31, with 
TR at only 151.27 t⋅km-2⋅year-1, which is considerably lower than re­
ported in most previous studies of the KOE and its adjacent area eco­
systems (Jiang et al., 2010; Lin et al., 2013; Watari et al., 2019). This 
could be attributed to the lower biomass of secondary consumers. Those 
indicators also indicated that this ecosystem is currently disturbed to a 
large extent and the stability of the system is still weak. Given that the 
KOE is an oceanic ecosystem in open ocean area, its increase of vari­
ability primarily arises from fishing and environmental variability (Sivel 
et al., 2022).
The omnivory index (OI) of functional groups is calculated based on 
the variance in the trophic levels of their prey. Functional groups that 
feed on a single trophic level will have an OI approaches 0. As the OI 
approaches 1, the trophic ranges of prey are broader, and the complexity 
of the food web increases (Christensen et al., 2005). The low OI of most 
species in the KOE indicated that these species have a specialized diet, 
feeding mainly on specific species or a few functional groups. For 
example, salmon primarily fed on small fishes and crustaceans (Sakai 
et al., 2012). In the KOE ecosystem, functional groups with lower OI are 
predominantly found among both higher and lower trophic level fishes. 
For example, top predators such as sharks, tunas, and swordfish had a 
relatively high concentration of prey organism species (Kubodera et al., 
2007; Trujillo-Olvera et al., 2018) thus showing lower OI. Some small 
pelagic fishes, such as anchovy, Pacific sardine and Pacific saury, pri­
marily feed on zooplankton, resulting in lower OI. In contrast, cepha­
lopoda exhibited a higher OI. This is likely due to their diverse diet. 
Watanabe et al. (2004) demonstrated that the prey items of cephalopoda 
varied widely, ranging from zooplankton to large pelagic fish. Such 
highly generalized diet might also be the main reasons why it can be 
widespread in the ecosystem, had high capacity to adapt to environ­
mental changes, and became a major fishery species in the KOE (Katugin 
et al., 2014).
The specialized diet of most species in this study area resulted in a 
low complexity of the ecosystem and a simpler food web structure. 
Consequently, the system omnivory index (SOI) of the ecosystem was 
relatively low (0.20), similar to that of the offshore ecosystem in the KOE 
(Jiang et al., 2010; Lin et al., 2013). However, it should be noted that the 
low SOI in most offshore ecosystems may be partly caused by ecosystem 
degradation due to fishing pressure and environmental change 
(Heymans et al., 2016), while the SOI in open ocean ecosystems was 
probably due to the specialized diet of pelagic species.
4.4. Evaluation of overall model uncertainty
Based on the results of PREBAL, the model is considered reasonable, 
although some parameters require more complete updating in 
subsequent studies and further testing of the model. In a survey of 50 
mass-balanced food web models, Morissette et al. (2007) found that 40 
% of models had pedigrees ranging from 0.20 to 0.39, and 48 % ranged 
from 0.40 to 0.59. The pedigree of our Ecopath model is 0.581, and over 
99.8 % of the model input parameters were effectively analyzed, which 
is relatively high and reflects the quality of the input parameters. 
Additionally, sensitivity analysis results indicated that the model’s 
overall output values varied less than the range of variation of the input 
parameters. It could be due to the fact that most of our input parameters 
derive from referenced data. Therefore, we concluded that the Ecopath 
model of the KOE ecosystem can be used for strategic management 
considerations at the ecosystem level, similar to other ecosystem 
models. However, as most of the static ecosystem models, our model 
lacked validation with long-term data series. The data quality of the KOE 
Ecopath model may not be sufficient for tactical management consid­
erations (Watari et al. 2019).
The sensitivity analysis indicated that the changes in biomass and P/ 
B of functional groups affected all the five output indicators (TST; sum of 
all production, TP; calculated total net primary production, NPP; TPP/ 
TR; total primary production/total biomass, TPP/TB). TPP is mainly 
affected by biomass and P/B of phytoplankton, while total biomass (TB) 
is affected by the biomass of each functional group, so changes in B will 
have an impact on TPP/TB and TPP/TR. But biomass uncertainty has a 
greater impact on the model’s main results than P/B and Q/B, the same 
as other studies (Susini and Todd, 2021). Therefore, enhancing the ac­
curacy of biomass would improve the model’s accuracy. Different from 
most of other historical studies (Ainsworth and Walters, 2015), the field 
sampling information was used to compile the biomass data as a way to 
improve the quality of the model parameters as much as possible in this 
study. However, it is important to noted that our survey was conducted 
only once in 2023 due to constraints of ship availability and does not 
account for specific seasonal variations (Lam et al., 2008; Vilas et al., 
2019). The representativeness of biomass data for highly migratory fish, 
as well as the import and export of the ecosystem, also requires careful 
consideration given the once-a-year survey data (Ainsworth and Wal­
ters, 2015). The trawl used in this survey consisted of a 40 mm stretch 
cod-end mesh with large mesh at the trawl mouth, which was not 
well-suited for the collection of smaller non-economic and lower trophic 
level species. The biomass estimates for functional groups such as an­
chovy, small pelagic fish, and small mesopelagic fish did not accurately 
reflect the actual species abundance. As a result, all of these functional 
groups exhibited disproportionately high EE, highlighting the need to 
use models to estimate their biomass more accurately. Similarly, the two 
functional groups of cephalopoda face a similar issue, which is primarily 
due to their selectivity regarding fishing gears. Therefore, improving the 
quality of the basic input data for functional groups such as small fishes 
and cephalopoda is a key focus for model optimization (Coll et al., 
2013). Changes in resources of food organisms would directly or indi­
rectly impact the food composition of predators through the food web, 
ultimately leading to a restructuring of fishery resources (Shapiro et al., 
2022). Limited by the survey gear and schedule, we were unable to 
effectively estimate the abundance of the Pacific saury. The reason is 
that the biological characteristics of the Pacific saury, such as diurnal 
vertical distribution and highly migratory, make necessary a more spe­
cific sampling protocol than used here (Hua et al., 2019). Biomass and 
production estimates for Pacific saury were derived from the results of 
previous studies. Consequently, the uncertainty level of biomass of Pa­
cific saury functional group was slightly higher. The accuracy of pa­
rameters will be further enhanced in the future through a combination of 
various approaches and comprehensive data analyses, such as long-term 
fitting of survey data and calibrations of time-dynamic simulations 
within the Ecosim component of Ecopath-with-Ecosim that account for 
seasonal variations (Christensen et al., 2005; Susini and Todd, 2021).

5. Conclusion
Based on the primary biomass data from the high-seas fishery re­
sources survey in the KOE conducted by the research vessel Songhang of 
Shanghai Ocean University in 2023, we analyzed the structure and 
characteristics of the KOE ecosystem using the EwE model. The total 
system throughput (TST) and the total respiration (TR) of the KOE 
ecosystem were lower than those reported in most previous studies 
which covered more nearshore regions of the KOE. The total primary 
production/total respiration ration TPP/TR was as high as 7.31, 
exceeding the values reported in most other studies of the KOE and its 
coastal ecosystems. The system omnivory index (SOI) was low (0.20), 
indicating that each predator group tended to feed within a relatively 
narrow trophic level range and the ecosystem was structurally simple 
and vulnerable to external disturbances. Sensitivity analysis revealed 
that model-derived indices were sensitive to changes and uncertainty in 
functional group biomass. The field sampling information was utilized 
as the biomass data to maximize the quality of the model. This study 
offers a reference for ecosystem-based fisheries management in the open 
ocean waters of the KOE.
