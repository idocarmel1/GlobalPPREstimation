Appendix S1 – Ecopath model parameter derivation methodology



The Ecopath with Ecosim approach

We modeled the Warm Pool ecosystem using Ecopath with Ecosim software

(www.ecopath.org). Ecopath trophic models describe the static state of energy flows in a food

web that balances a group’s net production with all sources of mortality and migration

(Polovina 1984). The software has a dynamic capability, called Ecosim, that allows forecasting

of ecosystem responses to specific perturbations through time (Walters et al. 1997, Pauly et al.

2000). Ecosim re-expresses the static Ecopath model in a dynamic form, whereby the dynamics

and sensitivity of the model is largely controlled by the predator consumption rates and the

proportion of the prey that exist in a vulnerable state. Detailed description and discussion of

Ecopath and Ecosim can be found in Walters et al. (1997), Christensen and Pauly (1992) and

Christensen and Walters (2004).

      The Ecopath master equation (Equation 1) balances a functional group’s net production

with all sources of mortality, migration, or change for that group, such that the net production

of a functional group equals the sum of (1) the total mass (or energy) removed by predators

and fisheries, (2) the net biomass accumulation of the group, (3) the net migration of the group’s

biomass, and (4) the mass flowing to detritus.

from stock assessment models, the SEAPODYM spatial ecosystem and population dynamics

model (Lehodey et al. 2008), quantitative sampling from scientific surveys, the literature, or if

unknown, left to be estimated by Ecopath. The data sources and their preparation for estimating

each parameter for each functional group in the model are described in Table S1. Quantitative

biomass estimates of intermediate to lower trophic level groups is often a major weakness of

pelagic ecosystem models (see Olson and Watters 2003), largely due to the difficulty of

quantitatively sampling the standing biomass. Therefore, we used estimates of forage, and

phytoplankton biomass from a 2012 version of a SEAPODYM model characterised for

skipjack (Senina et al. 2008).

      P/Bi is the production/biomass ratio (year-1), equivalent to natural mortality (M) for an

unfished population, total mortality (Z) for a fished population (Allen 1971), or the inverse of

maximum age (ω). If no information was available for natural mortality, which is common, an

estimate was made using up to three empirical equations (Pauly 1980, Hoenig 1983, Jensen

1996) depending on the biological data available for a particular group.

      Fishing mortality (F) data was available from stock assessments of the primary target

species, but was not available for the majority of bycatch species. Therefore, conservative

estimates of F were made by assuming F = 0.8(M), which can be a reasonable proxy for fishing

mortality at, or below, MSY (Gabriel and Mace 1999). In these cases, initial P/B values were

allowed to be decreased if required during the balancing process since they were likely to be

overestimated.

      EEi is the ecotrophic efficiency; the fraction of the total production of a group utilized in

the system. For most forage groups (e.g. sardines) that are likely to be consumed by a large

number of predators there is likely to be only a small proportion of the biomass that is not

utilised by the system. Values of around 0.95 are reasonable in such cases (Christensen et al.

2009). In contrast, for large predators having few, if any, predators (e.g. large sharks), or very



                                                                                                 2
small, abundant and highly productive organisms (e.g. phytoplankton and zooplankton), only

a small percentage of the biomass is likely to be consumed equating to EE values of around

0.1-0.3.

      Q/Bj is the annual food consumption rate per unit biomass of j. This is the equivalent of

annual ration, or more widely available, the daily ration (in % body weight day-1) multiplied

by 365 days. This parameter was either derived from the literature (e.g. Olson and Galvan-

Magana 2002, Griffiths et al. 2009a), or estimated from von Bertalanffy parameters using the

empirical equation of Palomares and Pauly (1998).

      DCji is the proportion of prey i in the average annual diet of predator j, represented in

terms of percent wet weight. Diet data were primarily derived from stomach content analyses

undertaken by the SPC Oceanic Fisheries Programme (Ecosystem Monitoring and Analysis

Section) from samples collected by the observer programmes of the region. These data were

supplemented, where necessary, by published and unpublished dietary data collected in studies

undertaken in Australia and Papua New Guinea by the CSIRO (e.g. Griffiths et al. 2009a,

Kloser et al. 2009). In the absence of dietary information for cetaceans, we used relevant data

from Pauly et al. (1998b). Together, these data were used to develop the model’s initial diet

matrix (Table S2), which was modified during the balancing process to produce the model’s

final diet matrix (Table S3). The main difference between these two matrices is the lack of

information on the predation of high trophic level species, particularly billfish and sharks, in

the initial matrix. This is due to the difficulty in documenting such predation events from

stomach content analysis, given the likely low frequency of these types of predation evens (e.g.

sharks consuming swordfish). These predators require predation mortality to be exerted upon

them in the model—as they would in the real world—to prevent their populations from

unrealistically increasing perpetually. Therefore, we used expert opinion and anecdotal




                                                                                              3
accounts of predation events involving these species to justify the creation of predatory links

in the final diet matrix.

      Yi is the total fisheries catch of a group (expressed as t km-2) averaged across the model

region (i.e. Y = F*B), and includes both retained and discarded catch. These data were derived

from logbook and scientific observer data held by the SPC (e.g. Williams and Terawasi 2015)

(Table S4).

      Ei is the net migration of i (emigration less immigration) over the course of a year, which

was assumed to be zero (i.e. emigration = immigration); and

      BAi is the biomass accumulation rate of i (t km-2 year-1). This is a production term that

can be calculated if the biomass at the end of the year and the beginning of the following year

is known. Since Ecopath is generally used as a trophic ‘snap shot’ in time, BAi is often assumed

to be zero (i.e. no biomass accumulation), as it was in the current model, and biomass time

series data used in Ecosim (see below) to force dynamic changes in the base Ecopath model.

      The Ecopath model is a system with as many such linear equations as there are functional

groups. The energy balance (conservation of matter) within each functional group is ensured

with the second master equation (Equation 2):

      The dynamics and sensitivity of Ecosim models is largely controlled by the consumption

rates (Qji) (Equation 3), which are limited by the proportion of a given predator group’s prey

that exist in a vulnerable state. Prey vulnerability is controlled within the expression of

consumption rate by a user-specified (or calculated) transfer rate of prey movement between

vulnerable and invulnerable pools (vij and v’ij), thus representing the universal community

stabiliser of prey refugia. The consumption rate (Qji) expressed in Equation 4 includes the prey

vulnerability parameters.

aij is the rate of effective search for prey i by predator j, Ti and Tj are the relative feeding times

of prey i and predator j, Sij is the user-defined seasonal and long-term environmental forcing

effects, Mij is the non-trophic mediation forcing effects, and Dj represents the effects of prey

handling time by predators, which further limits consumption rates.

      Parameters vij and v’ij represent prey vulnerabilities—or the rate of exchange of biomass

between two prey behavioural states—a state in which all predators have full access to prey

                                                                                                    5
and a state in which prey have full refuge from predators. Prey use refugia in real ecosystems

and so not all prey biomass is vulnerable to predation at any given time, and predator-prey

relationships are limited by behavioural and physical mechanisms. Ecosim is designed to allow

the specification of the type of trophic control (Lotka-Volterra type vs. donor control) that

mediates any interaction in the food web. Maximum consumption rates are hypothesised, and

thus the rate of exchange of biomass (vij) that a predator normally exerts. For high prey

vulnerability (vij) the functional relationship approximates a mass-action flow, or Lotka-

Volterra type interaction implying a strong ‘top-down’ effect. For low prey vulnerabilities the

functional relationship approaches a donor-controlled (bottom-up) flow rate so vij is the

maximum possible instantaneous mortality rate that j can cause on i (Walters et al. 1997).

      Prey vulnerabilities can be specified by adjusting the proportion of prey in vulnerable

and invulnerable states (pools) via adjustment of the v values, which represents Lotka-Volterra

(top-down) type control and pure donor control. In the real world, this mixture of trophic

control is mediated by temporal or spatial refugia, or by the relative primacy of physical and

biotic forces in regulating communities, i.e. predator-prey interactions. The vulnerability value

for each predator-prey interaction was estimated by fitting the Ecosim model to time series of

biomass, fishing mortality, and catch for functional groups where these data were available

(see Appendix S3 “Calibrating the Ecosim model”).

Appendix S2 – Balancing the Ecopath model



Balancing the Ecopath model

Once all model parameters were estimated from the available datasets and literature for 2005,

and multi-stanza models were constructed for swordfish, bigeye tuna, yellowfin tuna and

skipjack, balancing began by first focusing on the 14 functional groups for which high quality

age structured stock assessments had been undertaken.

      The model was attempted to be balanced to inspect the preliminary ecotrophic efficiency

(EE) and production/consumption estimates (P/Q) estimated by Ecopath for these key species.

For most living groups, the primary aim during balancing is to allow Ecopath to estimate an

EE of around 0.95 for species that are either heavily fished, or are expected to experience high

predation rates (Christensen et al. 2009). A value of around 0.95 indicates that 95% of the

biomass of the group is utilised within the system. In contrast, highly abundant and/or highly

productive species (e.g. zooplankton), or high trophic level predators that have very few natural

predators are expected to have low EE values of 0.1–0.3. This indicates that only a small

percentage of the species’ biomass is utilised within the system, while the remainder is

transferred to detritus or exported from the system.

      In cases where the EE value was unrealistic—for example juvenile skipjack initially had

an EE of over 1000—the biomass estimate was checked, followed by an inspection of the

predation mortality and fishing mortality estimates calculated by Ecopath. Commonly, the

most scientifically defensible option to reduce the EE of a group—when all other parameters

appear plausible—is to slightly reduce the proportion it contributes to the diets of predators

that have a very high biomass or are highly productive. The converse applies where the EE is

too low, requiring greater predation mortality when all other parameter values are considered

valid. These are common approaches used by Ecopath practitioners since the diet composition


                                                                                              32
of most marine predators is often highly variable in space and time and an Ecopath model

attempts to capture this variability for a single year. In many respects the initial diet

composition of a group should be viewed as a reasonable starting point with an expectation of

fine tuning using knowledge of the species and the system (see Christensen et al. 2009).

      After the EE values were considered reasonable, the P/Q estimate for each group was

checked to ensure it was biologically realistic. In most cases, P/Q should be between 0.1 and

0.3. In cases where P/Q was unrealistic, such as for Wahoo initially having a P/Q of 0.001, the

P/B and Q/B values were inspected and revised appropriately. It was often the case that daily

ration estimates from the literature were underestimated or unreliable, while the P/B value was

underestimated due to poor von Bertalanffy growth parameters from which natural mortality

(M) was estimated, or a lack of fishing mortality information, which was generally the case for

bycatch species. Further searches of the literature or sourcing unpublished work or expert

opinion often provided more reliable information that assisted in producing more reasonable

P/B estimates.

      Once the aforementioned process was completed for the groups having the most reliable

information, and then applied sequentially to prey of these groups occupying lower trophic

levels, and so on.



Diagnostics of Ecopath model validity and stability

On completion of the balancing process, we followed ‘best practice’ (Heymans et al. 2016)

and “PREBAL” (Link 2010) diagnostics to test the biological and ecological validity and

overall stability of the balanced model, which is summarised in Figure S1. The primary ‘rule

of thumb’ proposed by Link (2010) is that biomass of functional groups in the model should

span 5-7 orders of magnitude and decline at a rate of around 5-10% with increasing trophic

level. Figure S1a shows the biomass of functional groups in the Warm Pool model ranging



                                                                                            33
from 0.000057 t km-2 to 10.95 t km-2—slightly more than 6 orders of magnitude. Biomass

decreased with increasing trophic level at a rate of 22%, with values of the majority of groups

being close to the regression line.

      Both P/B and Q/B values are expected to decline with increasing trophic level (Link

2010, Heymans et al. 2016), which was the case for both parameters in the Warm Pool model

(Figure 1Sb and 1Sc).

      Finally, the gross food conversion efficiency (P/Q) of each functional group should only

produce a fraction of the biomass it has consumed (Heymans et al. 2016), usually on the order

of 0.05-0.3 (Christensen et al. 2009). All functional groups had P/Q values of between 0.05-

0.3 with the exception of four groups, which represent special cases. Seabirds and Sea turtles

had a P/Q of 0.01 and 0.026, respectively since their production is quite low. Seabirds, such as

albatross, have a very low natural mortality rates (i.e. P/B) (Tuck et al. 2001) due to high

parental investment, but have high consumption rates (Q/B) to satisfy their high metabolic

demands, thus resulting in a very low P/Q value. Similarly, Sea turtles are long-lived and also

have a low natural mortality, but have a reasonably high Q/B due to the consumption of low

energy prey, primarily gelatinous zooplankton. In contrast, “Mesopelagic fish and

crustaceans” and “Migratory mesopelagic fish and crustaceans” had slightly higher than

expected P/Q values (0.32-0.34) owing to their very high productivity and biomass that is

required to sustain very high predation mortality from a range of open ocean predators as a

‘wasp waist’ group (Griffiths et al. 2013), as is indicated by the high EE values for these groups

of 0.988 and 0.993, respectively.

      Once we confirmed that the underlying biological and ecological assumptions of the

Ecopath model had been satisfied using the recommendations of Link (2010) and Heymans et

transferred to Ecosim and run for 1000 years in the absence of fishing to assess the model’s

confirmed that the Ecopath model was robust and was ready to be used for forecasting changes

to the structure of the Warm Pool ecosystem under specific fishery and/or environmental

scenarios specified in Ecosim.



Appendix S3 – Calibrating the Ecosim model



Fitting the Ecosim model to time series data

Once the Ecopath model was confirmed to be internally stable and ecologically realistic, we

sought to optimise the reliability of predictions in Ecosim scenario modelling by calibrating

the model to 111 data time series consisting of biomass, and/or fishing mortality and/or catch

data for 37 of the 44 living functional groups. The data types used and their sources are detailed

in Table S5. Although several data time series for economically important species extended

from 1952 to 2010, data for years prior to around 1980 were considered unreliable due to

various data reporting issues. Therefore, we used the years 1952–1980 for essentially ‘burning

in’ the Ecosim model but focus on the years after 1980.

      The data for the 37 functional groups were imported into Ecosim and weighted according

to the perceived reliability of the data and its relative importance in driving the model fitting.

The 30 most sensitive interactions between predators and prey were identified using the non-


                                                                                               36
linear search procedure within Ecosim. The prey vulnerability rate (v, see Equation 4) for the

most sensitive predator-prey interactions were then iteratively adjusted by 1% until the sums

of squares error (SS) was minimised to produce the ‘best’ model fit to the time series data. This

process was repeated 20 times—with v values being reset before each iteration— to minimise

the chance of the nonlinear search procedure becoming ‘trapped’ in local parameter minima.

The matrix of vulnerability values by predator/prey interaction that contributed to the fit with

the lowest SS from the 20 iterations was saved as the ‘optimal’ vulnerability matrix, which is

shown in Table S6.

      In searching for the best combination of vulnerability values, the time series data were

linked to an estimated trend of primary productivity anomalies, in the form of a forcing

function, forced upon the “Large phytoplankton” group. A variance value of 10 was used for

the model fitting, implying that the model should attempt to capture any abrupt variability in

the biomass and catch data through time. This procedure did not reduce the SS and so the

forcing function was removed and not included in any of the final Ecosim simulations.

      Once the optimal vulnerability search was complete, vulnerability values (v) were

inspected to ensure they were ecologically realistic. Although some adjustments to

vulnerabilities and feeding time parameters often resulted in better visual fits of the model to

observed data, they often resulted in a poorer statistical fit (lower SS) or required unrealistic

parameter values, so they were disregarded. Further detailed descriptions of fitting Ecosim

models to time-series data can be found in Christensen et al. (2005).

      We were also interested in better understanding whether the Ecosim model was improved

by including bycatch time series data, since it is common to only have reliable time series for

commercially important species, which have generally been subjected to some type of stock

assessment. We assessed the influence of bycatch data by removing it from the data used to

calibrate the model, and recalibrated the model 10 times, as described above, and selected the



                                                                                              37
best model with the lowers SS. Our results showed that the mean SS for fits where bycatch was

removed was lower (SS = 52053 ± SD 1688) than for fits made using all data (SS = 55809 ±

SD 1832). Although removal of bycatch data resulted in a better statistical fit, owing to fewer

parameters being estimated, the quality of the model fits to observed data were substantially

worse. Figure S2 shows the model fits to observed data using all data and where bycatch data

were removed for four target species representing different trophic levels, and two commonly

caught shark species. Clearly, the fits to observed data for most species were degraded

following bycatch data removal, especially for Large bigeye tuna and Large skipjack. When

the bycatch data was removed, the biomass predictions of the two shark bycatch species varied

significantly in magnitude and trend.

Mixed Trophic Impact

Mixed Trophic Impact (MTI) is a sensitivity analysis within Ecopath that allows the strongest

trophic interactions to be identified. This can be useful both in terms of understanding important

trophic relationships, but also for identifying any possible errors or anomalies that may have

occurred during model construction or the model balancing process. Results of MTI are best

represented graphically—owing to the large number of combinations of predator-prey

interactions—and are shown in Figure 2 in the main manuscript.



Keystone groups

The MTI analysis primarily identifies the strongest trophic interactions as a result of abundant

and/or productive species preying upon species with lower biomass and productivity. However,

some of the most important ecological interactions can occur when species that have relatively low

biomass have an important predatory role in structuring an ecosystem. These species are often

considered “keystone” species and can have a disproportionally large influence on the structure of

the ecosystem relative to their biomass (Paine 1995). Results from Ecopath’s “Keystoneness”

analysis for the Warm Pool model are given in Table S7, showing Ecopath’s keystone index for

each functional group and its relative total impact on the ecosystem.

Ecological indicators

Fulton et al. (2005) undertook a simulation study using two ecosystem models and found that

indicators at the community or ecosystem level were the most reliable indicators of ecosystem

change under fishing. They found no single indicator was able to provide a complete representation

of the ecosystem and suggest that simultaneous use of a variety of indicators is likely to be required

to detect the full range of impacts from fishing.

      Fulton et al. (2005) found that useful ecosystem indicators from Ecopath are the Fishing in

Balance index (FIB) and the average trophic level of the catch (TLc). In a recent Ecopath modelling

study investigating the effects of fisheries and climate change on the northwest Pacific marine

ecosystem, Ainsworth et al. (2011) found that Kempton’s Q index adapted for ecosystem models

(see Ainsworth and Pitcher 2006) was a particularly useful indicator for assessing changes in the

diversity and biomass of high trophic level species (>TL 3). Such an indicator is highly relevant to

the Warm Pool ecosystem since the pelagic fisheries in the region target large predatory tunas,

billfish and tuna-like species. Therefore, FIB, TLc and Kempton’s Q were three indicators of

ecosystem change chosen for use in scenario modelling of the Warm Pool model. A description of

each indicator is provided below.

The FIB index (Pauly et al. 2000) provides an indication whether fisheries are balanced in

ecological terms and not causing disruption to the functionality of the ecosystem. FIB is defined

for any year i as:

Kempton’s Q index describes the slope of the cumulative species log-abundance curve and

Pitcher 2006). This was considered a particularly useful indicator for assessing ecosystem changes

as targets or bycatch in the Warm Pool fisheries generally occupy trophic levels higher than 3. An

increase in the index indicates an increase in upper level evenness and richness (Kempton and
